SplitSync

Real-time expense splitter with debt simplification, append-only edit history and exactly-once writes.

A trip starts with shared expenses. It ends with a tangle of IOUs. SplitSync finds the fewest transfers, keeps every change, and brings every open screen up to date.

Useful on a trip. Interesting under pressure.

Small details. Strong guarantees.

Splitting a bill is the easy part. Keeping the books correct when people retry, edit and arrive at once is where SplitSync earns its place.

Fewest transfers

The demo starts with 10 pairwise IOUs. Exact simplification finds 3 transfers; greedy needs 4.

Exactly-once payments

Ten devices recording the same payment: 1 commit, 9 stale-version responses. Retries replay the original receipt.

No lost updates

A room row lock serializes writers. Atomic balance changes and a ledger integrity check keep the cache honest.

Paise-exact splits

₹100 ÷ 3 = ₹33.34 / ₹33.33 / ₹33.33. The extra paisa rotates by expense id.

Edit history & one-tap undo

An edit adds a reversal and a new version. A deleted expense can be restored with the same shares.

Two people, one expense

Ten edits of the same version: 1 applied, 9 × 409 EXPENSE_CHANGED. No silent overwrite.

Live on every screen

Versioned snapshots cross Uvicorn workers over Redis Pub/Sub, then reach each open WebSocket.

Fails open

If Redis goes down, writes still stay exactly-once in PostgreSQL. Polling heals cross-worker freshness.

Architecture

Postgres owns correctness.

One worker commits the write. Redis carries the update to every worker, and each worker tells its own WebSocket clients. Redis is coordination only.

Uvicorn × 2 workers

FastAPI handles requests. Writers lock the room first, then member rows in ascending id order.

PostgreSQL 16

Integer-paise ledger, cached balances, row locks, unique constraints and prefix search.

Redis 8

Pub/Sub, rate-limit counters and an expiry-sweep leader lock. Every Redis call fails open.

WebSockets

Each worker pushes complete, versioned snapshots to its own clients.

Database

Every change leaves a record.

The ledger is append-only. Cached member balances update atomically in the same transaction; integrity queries recompute them from active ledger rows.

A protected settlement, in order

  1. Lock the room row.
  2. Check the idempotency key inside the lock.
  3. Check the expected graph version.
  4. Lock and validate the two members.
  5. Apply atomic balance deltas.
  6. Bump the room version.
  7. Insert the settlement and its receipt, then commit.

Algorithms

Fast is useful. Fewest is different.

The demo was chosen to show the gap: greedy needs four transfers, while the exact solver finds three.

Two-heap greedy

O(N log N)

Match the largest debtor and creditor. Each step settles at least one person, so the plan has at most N−1 transfers.

  1. Sneha → Aarav₹5,000.00
  2. Karthik → Priya₹3,500.00
  3. Karthik → Rohan₹1,500.00
  4. Sneha → Rohan₹1,000.00
Exact bitmask DP

O(2ⁿ · n)

Maximize disjoint zero-sum groups. Minimum transfers = non-zero members minus the number of groups. Exact runs for at most 15 non-zero balances.

  1. Karthik → Aarav₹5,000.00
  2. Sneha → Priya₹3,500.00
  3. Sneha → Rohan₹2,500.00
Zero-sum group recurrence
dp[mask] = max(dp[mask without i] for i in mask)
           + (1 if sum(mask) == 0 else 0)
minimum transfers = n − dp[full]

The left graph is expenses-only: IOUs as incurred. The right graph comes from current net balances and changes as payments are recorded.

Concurrency

Three checks. Three different jobs.

The row lock

Stops two writers being inside the room’s transaction at once. It works across workers and machines.

The version check

Rejects a decision made from an old screen. Settlements check the whole graph; edits check their expense version.

The idempotency key

Recognizes the same request after it commits and returns the original receipt. A changed body gets 422.

Real-time

A complete picture, with a version.

Versioned snapshots

Every write increments graph_version. Clients discard snapshots that are not newer than their current state.

Reconnect with backoff

1 · 2 · 4 · 8 · 16 · 30 s

Reconnects re-fetch the room before continuing live updates.

A quiet repair loop

A re-fetch every 30 s heals dropped messages or Redis downtime. Lost freshness never changes committed money.

Security & trust

Private by link. Honest about access.

There is no login. Anyone with the unguessable room link can add, edit or delete. History preserves changes, and “I am” is always self-reported.

Bounded inputs

Strict request models, a 16 KB body limit, integer money and composite foreign keys that prevent cross-room references.

Safe rendering

User text enters the page through textContent. A Content Security Policy blocks inline scripts; WebSocket handshakes check the Origin.

Diagrams

Design decisions

Sixteen choices behind the ledger.

The rationale from the master guide. Open any decision to see the trade-off.

Money is integer paise.

Amounts arrive as strings ("100.50"), are parsed with Decimal, and stored as BIGINT paise. No float touches money, so "balances sum to zero" holds exactly.

Largest-remainder splits.

Each share is floor(total × weight ÷ Σweights); leftover paise go to the largest fractional remainders. Ties rotate by expense id, so nobody always pays the extra paisa. Every share lands within 1 paisa of its exact proportion (property-tested on 2,000 random splits).

The ledger is the truth; balances are a cache.

Expenses, splits, settlements and reversals are append-only facts. Each member's net_balance_paise updates in the same transaction for fast reads, and integrity queries recompute it from the ledger.

Greedy for speed, exact for truth.

The two-heap greedy runs in O(N log N) with at most N−1 transfers, but it is not optimal: on the demo trip greedy needs 4 transfers and the exact bitmask DP (O(2ⁿ·n)) finds 3. Exact runs when at most 15 people have a non-zero balance — measured 3.7 ms at 12 and 35.6 ms at 15 — and above that the plan is greedy and labelled so.

The "before" graph is expenses-only.

Applying settlements pairwise breaks it: paying the optimal plan still leaves 10 arrows that cancel per person (verified). So the left graph shows IOUs as incurred, from live expenses; the right graph shows what's left, from net balances.

Postgres is the correctness layer.

Every write locks its group row (SELECT … FOR UPDATE), re-checks the idempotency key inside that lock, checks the relevant version, then changes balances with atomic SQL. Unique constraints back each rule even if the code has a bug.

Lock and version check do different jobs.

The lock stops simultaneous writes; the version check stops stale writes made from an old screen. A lock alone would still accept the same payment recorded by the other person a second later.

Redis is coordination, never correctness.

Pub/Sub fans WebSocket updates out across Uvicorn workers, counters rate-limit the public site, and a token-checked SET NX PX lock lets exactly one worker run the room-expiry sweep. If Redis dies, all three fail open and Postgres still guarantees exactly-once.

WebSockets carry versioned snapshots.

Each message is the full state plus graph_version; clients drop anything older than what they have. They reconnect with backoff and re-fetch on reconnect and every 30 s, so a lost message costs freshness, never correctness.

Two kinds of room.

A demo room is seeded, small (12 members, 200 expense versions, 500 settlements), deleted after 24 h idle, and has the chaos panel and reset. A group starts empty, holds 50 members, 10,000 expense versions and 10,000 settlements, is kept for 365 days after its last use, and never offers naive mode or reset.

Standard HTTP semantics.

PUT to edit, DELETE to delete, Idempotency-Key with the IETF draft's codes (400 missing, 422 reused with a different body, 409 conflict), and RFC 9457 Problem Details for every error.

Edits and deletes are reversal entries.

An edit writes, in one transaction, a reversal of the old version and a new version linked to it; a delete writes a reversal; a restore writes a copy. Nothing is overwritten, so the history and undo come for free and the integrity check still sees every rupee.

Edits are checked per expense.

You edit the version id on your screen. If someone changed that expense first, you get 409 EXPENSE_CHANGED naming the current version — but an unrelated change elsewhere in the room never blocks you. UNIQUE (revision_of) and a unique reversal target are the database backstop.

graph_version is the history clock.

Every write bumps it once and stamps its rows with it, so the history feed sorts by version and pages with a keyset cursor — never OFFSET — reading at most 4 × (limit + 1) rows per page.

Search is scoped, simple and prefix-based.

Postgres full-text search with the simple config, each word a prefix, within one room. No GIN index: the room's btree index narrows to at most 10,000 rows first, and searches measured 10–42 ms at that size.

Limits come from a load test, not guesses.

A full group (50 members, 9,900 expense versions, 9,900 settlements) built its snapshot in a median 154 ms, wrote in 22.5 ms and paged history in 9 ms. A database-size guard, not the room count, protects the disk.

Limitations

The edges are part of the design.

  • Redis downtime pauses cross-worker live updates. The 30-second re-fetch repairs freshness.
  • Plain HTTP/1.1 browsers may send only six requests at once, so naive browser chaos can show less than ±₹45,000 drift.
  • “I am” is self-reported. Anyone with the room link can edit; history keeps every change.
  • The exact solver stops at 15 non-zero balances. Bigger groups get the greedy plan, labelled honestly.
  • SplitSync records payments. It never moves money.
